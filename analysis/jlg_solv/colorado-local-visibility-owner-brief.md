# Johnson Law Group — Colorado local map visibility, 2026 year to date

Prepared 18 September 2026. Covers the five Colorado Google Business Profile locations.
Data: Local Brand Manager grid scans (11 Feb – 14 Sep 2026) and Google Business Profile
performance (1 Jan – 14 Sep 2026), via the SterlingX warehouse. The current SEO
engagement began in **March 2026**, which matters for reading every figure below.

## The short version

Measured from the start of the engagement (2 March) to now:

- **Fort Collins improved substantially** — map footprint 51% → 71%, average position
  15.8 → 14.9. The clearest gain in Colorado.
- **Englewood is at its best point of the year**, 27% top-three share in September, just
  ahead of March's 26%.
- **Commerce City is flat at the ceiling** — 89–94% all year, no trend.
- **Colorado Springs peaked in March and has drifted down since** — month average 9.1% in
  March, 7.6% now.
- **Denver has declined badly** — footprint 25% in February to about 7% now.

Two apparent gains are **not attributable to the engagement**: Fort Collins' jump from
21.5% to 51.3% and Colorado Springs' from 3% to 9% both occurred between the 11 February
and 2 March scans, at or before the takeover, and both are measured against a single
February scan.

Separately, contact actions from these listings fell 18% between comparable windows, and
calls fell 14%. That decline is real and is not explained below.

## How to read the numbers

The tool drops a 13x13 grid of points across each office's market and checks, from each
point, whether the firm appears in Google's top three map results. Three measures:

- **Top-three share** — percentage of grid points where the firm makes the top three.
  This is the headline "share of local voice."
- **Map footprint** — percentage of grid points where the firm appears *at all*.
- **Average position** — where it ranks at the points where it does appear.

**Top-three share has a hard cut-off, and that matters.** A location can grow its
footprint enormously and improve its position while top-three share stays near zero,
because third place is a cliff edge. Fort Collins is exactly that case: judged on
top-three share alone, its best result of the year reads as no change.

Two precision limits, both real:

- Top-three share is recorded in **whole percentage points**, so any location below about
  1% is at the measurement floor. Fort Collins' and Denver's top-three figures mean
  "effectively zero" with no readable trend.
- Footprint becomes **unstable when average position is poor**. At position ~17.5 (Fort
  Collins in February) a half-point measurement wobble moves footprint by ±14 points. At
  position ~15 (Denver) the July step corresponds to roughly 13 grid-point appearances out
  of 169. Treat footprint as reliable for large moves, not small ones.

## Location by location

Top-three share shown for the three locations above the measurement floor; footprint shown
for Fort Collins and Denver, where top-three share is effectively zero throughout.

**Fort Collins — the one substantial improvement.** From the engagement's start its
footprint rose from 51.3% to 71.2%, measured across roughly fourteen scans, with average
position improving 15.8 → 14.9. The honest caveat: position ~15 is still far outside the
top three, so almost none of this presence converts yet — all six tracked searches sit
between 0% and 2.5% top-three share. Real progress, one significant step short of paying
off. Its February baseline of 21.5% is a single scan at the least stable point of the
measurement range and should not anchor the claim.

**Englewood — strongest month, not a breakthrough.** September's 27% average is its best
of 2026, but March averaged 26% and peaked at 28% on a single scan, so this is a return to
and slight improvement on where it was six months ago. Position improved 9.4 → 8.7.

**Commerce City — at the ceiling, holding.** 99% footprint, position 1.8, 89–94% top-three
share all year with no trend. September's 92% sits inside April's 90–94% range. No
headroom. A result to protect, not improve.

**Colorado Springs — peaked in March, drifting down since.** It rose from 3% to a March
month-average of 9.1% (best single scan 10.8% on 2 March), and has since run between 5.5%
and 7.6%, currently 7.6%. Most of the rise predates the engagement and the direction since
has been mildly negative. Its footprint has not grown this year.

**Denver — the problem.** Denver has never registered top-three share in 2026, and its
footprint fell from 25% in February to about 7% now.

## Why Denver declined

Denver's footprint did not fall in one event. The full series:

| Feb–Mar | Apr | Jun | 6–13 Jul | 20 Jul onward |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ~25% | 18.5% | ~15% | 15.1% | **7.1% → 7.4%** |

So roughly **40% of the total decline happened before July**, gradually, and then a sharp
second step in mid-July. Any explanation has to account for both.

On the 13 July scan, the Englewood listing changed from a street address to a **Service
Area Business** — a listing type that hides the street address. Denver's footprint halved
on the very next scan.

Two checks were run on this, and both support a Denver-specific cause:

- **Only Denver moved.** Between 13 and 20 July, Denver's footprint fell 7.9 points while
  all seven other locations moved by 0.6 points or less. Third-party trackers recorded
  significant Google ranking volatility around 11–12 July 2026, so a market-wide update was
  a real alternative — but a market-wide update would have moved more than one location.
  This check rules it out as the explanation.
- **Denver's own listing did not change.** Its place ID, address, store code and business
  name are identical across all 89 scans from February to September, so the drop is not a
  Denver address edit or relisting. Google Business Profile changes that this data does not
  record — categories, hours, services, website URL — remain unchecked.

**What is not established is the mechanism, and one correction matters here.** Converting
to a Service Area Business does **not** broaden a listing's ranking reach. Google still
calculates distance from the hidden physical address, and naming a city as a service area
does not produce rankings there. So the intuitive "it now covers a wider radius" story is
wrong. The plausible mechanism is different: Google generally shows only one listing per
business for a given search, and an address-less Englewood listing whose service area
covers Denver may now win that filter for Denver-area searches. **That is speculation.** It
is consistent with the timing and with only Denver moving, but the mechanism has not been
demonstrated, and Englewood's own footprint did not rise when Denver's fell.

One further caveat: the two grids are about 11 miles apart, but the data records no grid
radius or point spacing, so how much they physically overlap is unverified.

**The action this forces is worth taking regardless of the mechanism:** confirm whether the
Englewood conversion was intentional. Englewood is a real office at 385 Inverness Parkway,
and an address-less listing for a real office is usually a mistake — it can also result
from a failed address verification. One caution: Englewood's best reading of the year was
measured *under* the Service Area configuration, so reversing it may not preserve that
result. The monthly series argues the conversion did not cause the gain — July and August
were both down after it, with the rise only in September — but this is worth watching.

**Important expectation-setting.** Even if Denver's footprint fully recovers, it will not
produce top-three visibility on its own. Denver held 25% footprint in February with **0%**
top-three share, at the same average position it has now. Restoring footprint restores
presence in the results, not placement in the pack. Denver needs prominence work —
authority, relevance, engagement — to convert presence into pack placement.

## What happened to calls and contacts

Comparing equal three-month windows, February–April against May–July:

| Measure | Feb–Apr | May–Jul | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calls | 193 | 166 | **−14%** |
| Website clicks | 584 | 452 | −23% |
| Direction requests | 842 | 705 | −16% |
| **All contact actions** | **1,619** | **1,323** | **−18%** |
| Profile impressions | 14,306 | 17,227 | +20% |

Note that direction requests are just over half of the total, so "contact actions" is not
a calls measure. Calls are broken out above and are down 14%.

**The decline is real and is not explained by the impression growth.** If the original
14,306 impressions had held their original rate they would have produced 1,619 actions.
Only 1,323 occurred. Even crediting the 2,921 additional impressions with *zero* actions,
the pre-existing volume must have produced fewer actions than before. So the "more
low-quality impressions diluted the rate" story cannot account for the roughly 296 missing
actions. Something reduced actions on volume that was already there.

**The impression figure itself is unreliable, and that is a separate finding.** Monthly
Colorado action rate:

| Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11.5% | 10.0% | 12.5% | 11.3% | **7.0%** | 8.7% | 7.6% |

This is a **step, not a slide** — roughly 11.3% average through April, roughly 7.8% from
May, with the break at the April–May boundary, where impressions also jumped 41% in a
single month. A discrete break of that shape is the signature of a change in what Google
counts as an impression, not of gradually worsening performance. Google did make
substantial changes to Business Profile performance reporting during 2026, including
counting impressions from new AI surfaces. If the denominator changed on 1 May, the two
windows are not comparable and the "action rate down a third" figure should not be treated
as a performance result.

**Two things this rules out.** The break does not align with the March takeover — March
(12.5%) and April (11.3%) were both *above* January, so the first two months of the
engagement were the strongest of the year on this measure. And the August–September data
gaps are not the cause: the feed is clean through July, verified per location, so the
May–July window is not understated.

**What remains unexplained** is the 18% absolute fall in actions and the 14% fall in calls.
Normal seasonality accounts for part of it — family law enquiries conventionally decline
from Q1 into summer, and excluding January does not remove that. There is no prior year in
this data to size it. Treat the absolute decline as real and partly unexplained; treat the
impression growth and the action-rate figure as unreliable.

## On reviews

| Location | Reviews | Rating | Footprint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Denver | 224 | 4.0 | ~7% |
| Commerce City | 212 | 4.1 | 99% |
| Colorado Springs | 89 | 4.1 | 44% |
| Englewood | 50 | 4.2 | 63% |
| Fort Collins | 17 | 4.3 | 71% |

Review count is clearly **not the binding constraint in Denver**: the location with the
most reviews has the weakest footprint, and the location with the fewest has one of the
strongest. Denver also held 25% footprint in February on the same review count, so its
decline is not a review problem. That does not mean reviews do not matter — it means
Denver's problem is elsewhere, and a review campaign should not be the first move there.
This is five locations in one account, not a general rule, and the data cannot test the
more useful question of whether locations that *gained* reviews gained visibility, because
the review table carries no dates.

Ratings are a separate and legitimate concern. Every Colorado location sits between 4.0
and 4.3 stars, and 4.0 in the highest-demand market works against conversion even where
visibility is strong. Note this is served by the same review-generation work: the
recommendation is not "no reviews in Denver," it is "reviews will not restore Denver's map
visibility."

## What this data cannot tell you

- **It cannot confirm what caused any gain.** These figures show what changed and when.
  Matching that to work performed is a separate exercise.
- **Denver is the largest of these markets by population and search demand, but not by
  profile views** — Commerce City currently generates more impressions (5,981 vs 3,751).
  The market-size claim rests on external demand data not re-verified here.
- **February through May are snapshots, not months** — one scan day each for February,
  April and May, two for March. Weekly scanning began in June.
- **September is a half month**, through 14 September, and the locations that rose did so
  on the same two scan days.
- **No prior-year comparison exists.** Both sources begin in 2026.
- **August and September profile figures are incomplete** — 5 of 31 August days and 6 of 14
  September days lost across all locations — and were excluded from every comparison.
- **None of this measures signed clients.** Connecting visibility to retained matters
  needs the intake and CRM data.

## Recommended next steps

1. **Push Fort Collins from presence to top three.** It already appears in 71% of its
   market at position 14.9 and is the location closest to earning real top-three share.
   Roughly four positions of improvement would start converting — an estimate interpolated
   from other locations in different competitive markets, not a target.
2. **Confirm whether the Englewood Service Area Business conversion was intentional**, and
   restore the street address if not. Cheap, and correct on listing-integrity grounds
   whatever its effect on Denver. Expect it to restore Denver's grid presence, not its pack
   placement.
3. **Re-scan Denver and Englewood after any listing change** to test the suppression
   explanation directly.
4. **Diagnose Colorado Springs' inverted pattern** (below). Specific and unexplained.
5. **Plan Denver prominence work separately** from the listing fix, since footprint
   recovery alone will not produce top-three placement.
6. **Report calls and form fills, not impressions**, until the May measurement break is
   explained.
7. **Address ratings, starting with Denver.**
8. **Protect and monitor Commerce City.** No further map-visibility gains are available at
   99% footprint and position 1.8, though it remains the largest contributor of profile
   views and actions.

## Appendix: these offices only win when searchers name their city

Splitting each location's tracked searches into those containing the city name
("englewood divorce lawyers") and those not ("divorce lawyers"). All five Colorado
locations track the same structure — two city-name terms and the same two generic terms —
so this comparison is sound.

Top-three share, September:

| Location | City-name searches | Generic searches |
|---|---|---|
| Commerce City | 100% | 83% |
| Englewood | 54% | **0%** |
| Colorado Springs | **0.3%** | 15% |
| Fort Collins | 0.5% | 1% |
| Denver | 0% | 0% |

**Englewood's success is entirely city-name driven.** It takes 54% of top-three slots for
"englewood family lawyers" — appearing at every grid point at position 3.6 — and 0% for
"divorce lawyers," all year without exception. Its September gain came entirely from
city-name searches rising 38% → 54%: an improvement in a category it already owned, not
entry into the larger generic market.

**Colorado Springs is inverted, and nobody has explained it.** It takes 15% of top-three
slots on generic searches but essentially **zero** on "colorado springs divorce lawyers"
and "colorado springs family lawyers." It ranks around position 8 generically and position
15 on its own city name, consistently across sixteen scans. The likely explanation is that
explicit "colorado springs" searches return a pack anchored near the city centre while the
office sits in the northwest at 2139 Chuckwagon Road, so closer competitors win. This is
the most specific testable problem in the Colorado data.

**Only Commerce City competes on generic searches**, and it is the smallest of these
markets. In the metros generating the most enquiries the firm holds no top-three map
presence for unqualified "divorce lawyers" searches at all.
